Co-op Work Term Report: Shopify Developer at Helios Technology Solutions

I spent my first co-op term at Helios Technology Solutions moving product catalogues between commerce systems, writing Shopify tooling, and talking to the store owners on the other end of it. If you take one thing from this page, take this: almost everything I learned started with data that did not look the way I expected.

The employer

Helios; one of Shopify's retail launch partners

Helios Technology Solutions helps retailers move from legacy point-of-sale systems to Shopify. The company supports data migration, product catalogues, inventory, Shopify POS setup, and the integrations required for a successful launch. As one of two co-op students on a small team, I learned how to deliver software efficiently, communicate with merchants, and contribute to systems that people rely on every day. Moving the data was only the first step. The real goal was to give each merchant a Shopify setup they could trust on opening day.

Data migration was the minimum. The job was making something a merchant could trust on their opening day with Shopify. What I would tell a student starting the same placement

Goals and learning outcomes

What I set out to get better at, and where I actually landed

I set these four goals during my first week, before I fully understood what the role would involve. By the end of the term, I had achieved three and made meaningful progress on the fourth.

Work in real production code

I wanted experience contributing to an established codebase instead of always starting from an empty file. Learning to understand existing conventions, make targeted changes, and preserve working functionality became an important part of the term. Python · Pandas · GraphQL

Met

Diagnose problems I had not seen before

Academic exercises usually define the problem clearly. At work, I wanted to become comfortable investigating issues when the cause was unknown. This meant gathering evidence, testing my assumptions, and tracing problems across data and systems before deciding on a solution. Debugging · Testing · Data inspection

Met

Explain technical work to non-technical people

I expected this to be one of the most challenging goals, but it became one of the most valuable. Client calls taught me to avoid jargon, confirm that my explanation was clear, and always leave the client with a practical next step. Client support · Training · Documentation

Met

Design software, not just make it work

I became much better at delivering reliable fixes, but I am still learning to step back and evaluate the design around them. I also want to build stronger testing habits so potential problems are caught before they reach users. I will carry this goal into my next work term. Architecture · Testing discipline

Partly met

The work

Mostly migration and verification, some building

Most of my time was spent moving product data between systems and verifying that every part of the migration had worked correctly. I also supported store launches and contributed to an existing Shopify application.

Migrating and verifying catalogue data

This was my main responsibility. I used Python and Pandas to clean and reshape large product exports, GraphQL to load the transformed data into Shopify, and AI-assisted checks to identify issues that could be missed during manual review. These included mismatched categories, near-duplicate SKUs, and prices that did not match the source system. Transforming the data was only part of the challenge. I also had to verify that thousands of records had migrated correctly and identify the exceptions that required attention. The experience showed me that data migration is not simply copying fields from one system to another. It requires understanding business rules that may be undocumented, inconsistent, or known only by the people who use the system every day.

The lesson was that migration is not copying fields, but understaning what the data actually means, and how it is used in the real world.

Getting stores ready to open

I answered merchant questions, investigated setup issues, and guided clients through Shopify POS workflows before launch. Many concerns were not software bugs. Instead, they came from differences between how the system worked and how the merchant expected it to work.

Supporting clients directly helped me understand that usable software depends on clear expectations, effective communication, and workflows that make sense to the people using them.

Contributing to an existing Shopify app

In addition to the migration work, I contributed to an existing Shopify application that used the Admin GraphQL API. Much of this work involved understanding the codebase, identifying the patterns already in use, and making changes that followed those conventions.

Although this represented a smaller portion of my term, it taught me to consider maintainability and whether my code would be clear to the next developer who worked on it.

Receiving this review was especially meaningful because it showed that my work had a direct and positive effect on a merchant’s launch. It also reinforced how much I valued communicating with clients and helping them feel confident during a major system change.

Reflection

Three habits I did not have in April

01

I look at the data before I write the code

Before this term, my instinct was to start coding immediately. Now I begin by inspecting the data, sorting it, counting missing values, checking formats, and finding records that do not match my assumptions. Many of the most difficult bugs I encountered could have been prevented by taking a few minutes to understand the data first.

02

I explain things in the listener's terms

On a client call, being technically correct is not enough. The explanation also has to be clear and useful. I learned to use the client’s terminology, confirm their understanding, and finish each conversation with a specific next step.

03

I treat tools as one system, not separate subjects

Python scripts, dataframes, APIs, AI tools, and browser developer tools no longer feel like separate subjects. I became comfortable moving between them to investigate one problem from several angles. That adaptability is one of the most transferable skills I gained.

Conclusion

If you remember one thing

This work term connected what I had learned at university with the realities of building software for clients. I began the term focused on writing code that passed test cases. I finished it thinking more carefully about the people who use that code, what their data looks like, what they expect the system to do, and what happens if an issue is not caught.

My technical skills grew, but the larger lesson was learning how those skills work together in a real business environment. I am grateful for the mentorship, feedback, and trust I received at Helios, and I look forward to applying these lessons in my next work term.
